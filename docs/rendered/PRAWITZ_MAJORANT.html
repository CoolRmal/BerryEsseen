The pointwise inequality behind Prawitz smoothing

This note proves the pointwise majorant, its probability bounds, and the four-term Fourier smoothing inequality. All these steps are formalized in Lean, including endpoint integrability. The numerical certificate assembly is also complete; see the proof map.

The useful mechanism is a shift recurrence. Its sign becomes transparent after adding one elementary correction term.

1. Define the approximation

For 0 < s < 1, put

B(s)=(1−s)cot⁡(πs)+1π,H(x)=2∫01B(s)sin⁡(2πxs) ds.B(s)=(1-s)\cot(\pi s)+\frac1\pi, \qquad H(x)=2\int_0^1 B(s)\sin(2\pi xs)\,ds.B(s)=(1−s)cot(πs)+π1​,H(x)=2∫01​B(s)sin(2πxs)ds.

Although B has a singularity at zero, its product with the sine is integrable. For example, Jordan's sine inequality on the two half-intervals gives

∣B(s)sin⁡(2πxs)∣≤π∣x∣+2.|B(s)\sin(2\pi xs)|\le \pi|x|+2.∣B(s)sin(2πxs)∣≤π∣x∣+2.

The endpoint values of the integrand do not affect the integral. The function H is odd and H(0)=0. Define

S(x)=1+H(x)+sinc⁡(πx)22,sinc⁡(u)={sin⁡(u)/u,u≠0,1,u=0.S(x)=\frac{1+H(x)+\operatorname{sinc}(\pi x)^2}{2}, \qquad \operatorname{sinc}(u)=\begin{cases}\sin(u)/u,&u\ne0,\\1,&u=0.\end{cases}S(x)=21+H(x)+sinc(πx)2​,sinc(u)={sin(u)/u,1,​u=0,u=0.​

We will prove

1[0,∞)(x)≤S(x).\boxed{\mathbf 1_{[0,\infty)}(x)\le S(x).}1[0,∞)​(x)≤S(x).​

In particular, S(0)=1, as required for a right-continuous distribution function.

2. The shift recurrence

The sine difference identity cancels the cotangent denominator:

cot⁡(πs)[sin⁡(2π(x+1)s)−sin⁡(2πxs)]=cos⁡(2π(x+1)s)+cos⁡(2πxs).\cot(\pi s)\bigl[\sin(2\pi(x+1)s)-\sin(2\pi xs)\bigr] =\cos(2\pi(x+1)s)+\cos(2\pi xs).cot(πs)[sin(2π(x+1)s)−sin(2πxs)]=cos(2π(x+1)s)+cos(2πxs).

Only two elementary integrals remain:

∫01sin⁡(ks) ds=1−cos⁡kk,∫01(1−s)cos⁡(ks) ds=1−cos⁡kk2(k≠0).\int_0^1\sin(ks)\,ds=\frac{1-\cos k}{k},\qquad \int_0^1(1-s)\cos(ks)\,ds=\frac{1-\cos k}{k^2} \quad(k\ne0).∫01​sin(ks)ds=k1−cosk​,∫01​(1−s)cos(ks)ds=k21−cosk​(k=0).

Substitution and cancellation give, for x>0,

H(x+1)−H(x)=c(x)x2(x+1)2,c(x)=(sin⁡πxπ)2.\boxed{H(x+1)-H(x)=\frac{c(x)}{x^2(x+1)^2}, \qquad c(x)=\left(\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi}\right)^2.}H(x+1)−H(x)=x2(x+1)2c(x)​,c(x)=(πsinπx​)2.​

The same integrand identity at x=0 gives H(1)=1. Since c(n)=0 for every integer, we get H(n)=1 for every positive integer n.

3. The limiting value needs only Riemann–Lebesgue

Fix a real x. The difference formula gives

H(x+n)−H(n)=4∫01B(s)sin⁡(πxs)cos⁡(2πs(n+x/2)) ds.H(x+n)-H(n) =4\int_0^1 B(s)\sin(\pi xs) \cos\bigl(2\pi s(n+x/2)\bigr)\,ds.H(x+n)−H(n)=4∫01​B(s)sin(πxs)cos(2πs(n+x/2))ds.

Its amplitude B(s) sin(πxs) is integrable by the bound in step 1. The Riemann–Lebesgue lemma says that this integral tends to zero as the frequency tends to infinity. Because H(n)=1 for positive integers,

H(x+n)⟶1.H(x+n)\longrightarrow1.H(x+n)⟶1.

Thus the limiting value follows without evaluating an improper sine integral or summing a cotangent Fourier series. The Lean proof uses mathlib's Riemann–Lebesgue theorem, then takes real parts and restricts the amplitude to [0,1].

4. Two monotone sequences prove the majorization

Fix x>0, and write c=c(x)≥0. Integer shifts leave c unchanged. The recurrence shows that H(x+n) increases to 1; therefore

H(x)≤1.H(x)\le1.H(x)≤1.

Now add the correction term and compute its increment:

[H(x+n+1)+c(x+n+1)2]−[H(x+n)+c(x+n)2]=−2c(x+n)(x+n+1)2≤0.\begin{aligned} &\left[H(x+n+1)+\frac{c}{(x+n+1)^2}\right] -\left[H(x+n)+\frac{c}{(x+n)^2}\right]\\ &\hspace{25mm}=-\frac{2c}{(x+n)(x+n+1)^2}\le0. \end{aligned}​[H(x+n+1)+(x+n+1)2c​]−[H(x+n)+(x+n)2c​]=−(x+n)(x+n+1)22c​≤0.​

This corrected sequence decreases and bounds H(x+n) from above. Taking the limit of the latter proves

1≤H(x)+cx2=H(x)+sinc⁡(πx)2.1\le H(x)+\frac{c}{x^2}=H(x)+\operatorname{sinc}(\pi x)^2.1≤H(x)+x2c​=H(x)+sinc(πx)2.

Hence S(x)≥1 when x>0. For x<0, oddness and H(-x)≤1 give H(x)≥-1, so S(x)≥0. Finally, S(0)=1. This proves the boxed pointwise inequality.

5. Average it to bound a CDF

Let X have a finite first moment, let F(a)=P(X≤a), and choose any k>0. Pointwise majorization, applied at k(a-X), gives

F(a)≤ES(k(a−X)).F(a)\le \mathbb E S(k(a-X)).F(a)≤ES(k(a−X)).

The reflected inequality gives the other side:

1−ES(k(X−a))≤F(a).1-\mathbb E S(k(X-a))\le F(a).1−ES(k(X−a))≤F(a).

These expectations exist: the same integral bound yields

∣S(x)∣≤π∣x∣+3,|S(x)|\le\pi|x|+3,∣S(x)∣≤π∣x∣+3,

and S is measurable. The proof handles an atom at a explicitly; the upper pointwise bound equals one at zero and the reflected lower bound equals zero there.

The normal CDF representation

Fubini's theorem integrates the Gaussian cosine transform once:

∫sin⁡(xt)t dγ(t)=∫0x(∫cos⁡(ut) dγ(t))du=∫0xe−u2/2 du,\int \frac{\sin(xt)}t\,d\gamma(t) =\int_0^x\left(\int\cos(ut)\,d\gamma(t)\right)du =\int_0^x e^{-u^2/2}\,du,∫tsin(xt)​dγ(t)=∫0x​(∫cos(ut)dγ(t))du=∫0x​e−u2/2du,

where γ is the standard Gaussian probability measure. The product integrand is bounded by one, so the exchange is justified on every finite interval. The exceptional frequency t=0 is a Gaussian null set. Substituting the Gaussian density and then using evenness gives

Φ(x)−12=1π∫0∞e−t2/2sin⁡(xt)t dt.\Phi(x)-\frac12=\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty e^{-t^2/2}\frac{\sin(xt)}t\,dt.Φ(x)−21​=π1​∫0∞​e−t2/2tsin(xt)​dt.

The integral is absolutely convergent because |sin(xt)/t| ≤ |x|. The formal proof also includes the substitution t=Ts for every T>0, as used in the smoothing inequality.

The four integrals

Write K=K₁, g(t)=exp(-t²/2), and choose any T>0 and 0<τ≤1. On the low-frequency interval, the identity

K(s)f(Ts)−iπsg(Ts)=K(s)(f(Ts)−g(Ts))+(K(s)−iπs)g(Ts)K(s)f(Ts)-\frac{i}{\pi s}g(Ts) =K(s)(f(Ts)-g(Ts))+ \left(K(s)-\frac{i}{\pi s}\right)g(Ts)K(s)f(Ts)−πsi​g(Ts)=K(s)(f(Ts)−g(Ts))+(K(s)−πsi​)g(Ts)

separates the distributional error from the correction to the normal inversion kernel. Multiplication by exp(-iTsx) preserves norms. On [τ,1], bound the distributional term by |K(s)||f(Ts)|. The remaining normal integral on [τ,∞) is bounded using |sin(Tsx)|≤1. The reflected kernel has the same two norms because K₋₁=-conj(K₁) and K₋₁-i/(πs)=-conj(K₁-i/(πs)). Thus both CDF bounds give

∣F(x)−Φ(x)∣≤∫0τ∣K(s)∣ ∣f(Ts)−g(Ts)∣ ds+∫τ1∣K(s)∣ ∣f(Ts)∣ ds+∫0τ∣K(s)−iπs∣g(Ts) ds+∫τ∞g(Ts)πs ds.\boxed{ \begin{aligned} |F(x)-\Phi(x)|\le{}& \int_0^\tau |K(s)|\,|f(Ts)-g(Ts)|\,ds\\ &+\int_\tau^1 |K(s)|\,|f(Ts)|\,ds\\ &+\int_0^\tau\left|K(s)-\frac{i}{\pi s}\right|g(Ts)\,ds\\ &+\int_\tau^\infty\frac{g(Ts)}{\pi s}\,ds. \end{aligned}}∣F(x)−Φ(x)∣≤​∫0τ​∣K(s)∣∣f(Ts)−g(Ts)∣ds+∫τ1​∣K(s)∣∣f(Ts)∣ds+∫0τ​​K(s)−πsi​​g(Ts)ds+∫τ∞​πsg(Ts)​ds.​​

The endpoint at zero causes no convergence problem. Jordan's inequality and elementary Taylor bounds give |cot(πs)-1/(πs)|≤π²s/3 for 0<s≤1/2. Consequently, K(s)-i/(πs) stays bounded and s|K(s)| stays bounded. A finite first moment gives

∣f(Ts)−g(Ts)∣≤(∣T∣E∣X∣+T22)s(0≤s≤1).|f(Ts)-g(Ts)|\le \left(|T|\mathbb E|X|+\frac{T^2}{2}\right)s\qquad(0\le s\le1).∣f(Ts)−g(Ts)∣≤(∣T∣E∣X∣+2T2​)s(0≤s≤1).

The apparent singularity in the first integrand is therefore cancelled. Away from zero, the kernel norms are bounded; the last integral is dominated by a Gaussian divided by πτ. These facts justify all the splitting and integral comparisons above.
